Block 1: estimation and the confidence interval

Building an interval, then saying what it means

A different 34,434 people

  • Last week’s mean sedentary time, 6.56 hours/day, came from one sample
  • We don’t know how well this number represents the US population
  • A different 34,434 people would have given a different number. How different, and does it matter?

Population and sample

  • The population is what the question is about
  • The sample is what we can afford to measure
  • Everything we compute is computed on the sample
  • Everything we want to say is about the population

The parameter and the estimate

  • The estimand: the fixed, unknown population value the question is about
  • The estimator: the method that produces a number from a sample. It is random, because who ends up in the sample is random
  • The estimate: one particular result the estimator produced
  • What is fixed and what is random is the distinction the rest of this block depends on
  • One fixed target (navy), five rounds of sampling below it
  • Each round draws four samples. Their estimates (blue) scatter around the target, none exactly on it
  • Sample 1’s estimates are labelled x̄₁ to x̄₄: each is one sample’s own estimate

Random sampling, and what can go wrong

  • Random sampling is what licenses generalising from the sample to the population
  • Departures from it are bias
    • Who was reachable
    • Who agreed
    • Who was excluded

Two different problems: bias and imprecision

  • Bias. Aiming at the wrong target
    • Week 5’s selection bias is an example
  • Imprecision. Scatter around the right target
    • A confidence interval addresses this, and does nothing about bias
  • A confidence interval states its precision, the margin of error
    • That can look like accuracy, and is not necessarily accurate
    • Validity, being free of bias, cannot be inferred from a confidence interval

The sampling distribution

  • We report a confidence interval to state precision
    • The interval is built from the standard error
    • The standard error is the spread of the estimate
    • That spread is a property of the estimate’s own distribution
  • Imagine applying the same estimator to different random samples, repeatedly
  • The estimates themselves form a distribution: the sampling distribution
  • Everything from here to the confidence interval rests on this distribution

Seeing it happen

  • Two coins, fair (p = 0.5) and biased (p = 0.1), and four sample sizes: n = 1, 2, 5, 25
  • Buttons roll 1, 5, 20, 100 or 1000 samples, so the shape of the sample mean builds live
  • A run of n flips has a mean too: the proportion of heads in that run

What the demonstration shows

  • The fair coin’s parent distribution is symmetric: at n = 1 it is two equal spikes, at 0 and at 1
  • The biased coin’s parent distribution is skewed: at n = 1 most flips land at 0, and few at 1
  • Roll up n, for either coin, and the sample mean’s own distribution drifts toward a bell shape
  • The parent does not have to be normal. A skewed parent still ends up bell-shaped, it just needs a larger n to get there than a symmetric parent does
  • The skewed case that matters for this course is sed, right-skewed, in Chapter 4, which refers back to what this seminar showed

The central limit theorem

  • The distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal shape as n grows
  • Whatever the shape of the underlying data

Standard deviation and standard error

  • The most persistent confusion in the course. Same units, different subjects
  • Standard deviation (SD): how spread out the data are
  • Standard error (SE): how spread out the estimate is

\[SE = \frac{SD}{\sqrt{n}}\]

  • The SE inherits the SD, but shrinks with the square root of n: the estimate can be far more precise than the data are tight

Sedentary time: the standard error of its mean

Quantity Mean SD N SE
Sedentary time, hours/day 6.56 10.9 34,434 0.0588
  • Let it land before explaining it: an SE of 0.059 hours/day, from data spread over 10.9

Try it: the same study, a hundred times the size

Study Mean SD n
Study A 6.5 10.9 100
Study B 6.5 10.9 10000
  • Two studies report the same mean and the same SD, in different populations
  • Work out the standard error for each. What has changed between the two studies, and what has not?
  • Three to five minutes

Try it: the answer

  • Study A: SE = 10.9 / sqrt(100) = 1.09 hours/day
  • Study B: SE = 10.9 / sqrt(10,000) = 0.109 hours/day
  • The mean is identical and the SD is identical: both studies found the same average and the same spread of people
  • Only the precision of the estimate differs, by a factor of ten, because n went up a hundredfold and the SE divides by the square root
  • The data did not get tidier. The estimate got sharper. Precision is bought with n, and bought slowly

A proportion is a mean too

  • A proportion is a sample mean
    • Code the outcome 1 for everyone who has it, 0 for everyone who does not
    • The proportion with the outcome is exactly the mean of that column of 0s and 1s
    • Example: 3 of 5 patients improved. The column is 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, and its mean is 0.6, the same 0.6 we would call a proportion
  • The CLT applies to it for the same reason it applies to any other mean
  • Nothing new is assumed: the same theorem, pointed at a different kind of variable

The standard error of a proportion

\[SE = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}\]

  • The same SD-over-root-n shape as the formula for a mean, with sqrt(p(1-p)) playing the part of the SD
  • For a 0/1 variable, the spread is fixed by the proportion itself, and does not have to be estimated separately

A proportion and its standard error

Quantity Proportion improved N SE
Improved, streptomycin trial 0.514 107 0.0483
  • The 1948 MRC streptomycin trial: 55 of 107 patients improved

What a confidence interval is, and why we report one

  • An estimate on its own says nothing about how far off it might be, and how far off it might be depends on n
  • A confidence interval attaches precision to the estimate
    • It is why a result is reported as a range, not a point
    • It is built from the standard error
    • Its width is the margin of error, the amount of imprecision being admitted to
  • It is the estimate ± about two standard errors

Why 1.96, and when t replaces it

  • 1.96 comes from the normal distribution the CLT delivers
  • With a small sample we do not know the SD. We estimate it from the same small sample, so that estimate of spread is itself uncertain
  • The t distribution allows for that extra uncertainty
    • Same bell shape, heavier tails
    • The multiplier is bigger than 1.96, and grows as n shrinks
    • At n = 10 it is about 2.26. At n = 30, about 2.05
  • As n grows, t converges on the normal, and the multiplier returns to 1.96

A 95% interval for sedentary time

  • 6.56 ± 1.96 × 0.0588
  • 6.56 ± 0.12
  • 6.45 to 6.68 hours/day

A 95% interval for a proportion

Quantity Estimate 95% CI
Improved, streptomycin trial 0.514 0.419 to 0.609
  • 0.514 of patients improved (95% CI: 0.419 to 0.609)
  • Proportion-CI material lives in Week 8 only

Try it: an interval from scratch

Quantity Mean SD N
Age, years 47.5 18.5 41,765
  • Given the mean, SD and n above, find the standard error, then the 95% interval
  • Three lines, five minutes

Try it: the answer

  • SE = 18.5 / sqrt(41,765) = 0.091 years
  • Interval: 47.5 ± 1.96 × 0.091
  • 47.3 to 47.7 years

A range of compatibility

  • An interval is not a single verdict about one number
  • Every value inside it is a value the data are reasonably compatible with. Every value outside it is one the data sit poorly with
  • 6.45 to 6.68 hours/day says a population mean of 6.5 is compatible with what we saw, and so is 6.6. A mean of 7.2 is not
  • Compatibility is a matter of degree, not a line: values near the middle sit better than values near the ends
  • This is what makes an interval more informative than any test that only presents a p-value, which Week 9 covers

What a 95% confidence interval means

  • Of all the intervals this procedure could build, 95% of them would contain the true population value
  • The population value is fixed. It is the interval that moves from sample to sample

A common misconception

  • ‘We are 95% confident the true mean lies in this range’
  • This statement implies the population mean is random, something with a 95% chance of landing inside a fixed range
  • The intuitive objection: if 95% of all such intervals cover the true value, surely this one has a 95% chance of covering it too?
  • It does not. The population mean is fixed, and this one interval either contains it or does not. Once both are fixed, there is no probability left in the situation
  • The 95% is a property of the procedure, across repeated samples, not a property of this one interval
  • The practical way to avoid the confusion: write the result and let it stand, with no probability sentence attached
    • 6.56 hours/day (95% CI: 6.45 to 6.68)

Seeing coverage

  • Each click draws 1000 fresh 95% intervals against the true mean (navy line); navy bars miss it
  • Excludes 145 physically impossible values (over 24 hours/day). True mean here is 5.89 hours/day, not the 6.56 used elsewhere

This week’s methodological anchor

  • Greenland et al. (2016), European Journal of Epidemiology: ‘Statistical tests, P values, confidence intervals, and power: a guide to misinterpretations’
  • Names the specific wrong readings, not just the general warning
  • PubMed
  • Judging whether a sentence says what the numbers support is where half the assessment marks are

‘Misinterpretation and abuse of statistical tests, confidence intervals, and statistical power have been decried for decades, yet remain rampant.’

‘We then provide an explanatory list of 25 misinterpretations of P values, confidence intervals, and power.’

Try it: is this interpretation sound?

  • The sedentary-time interval: 6.45 to 6.68 hours/day
  • A. ‘We are 95% confident the true mean lies in this range’
  • B. ‘There is a 95% probability the true mean lies in this range’
  • C. ‘If we repeated this study many times, 95% of the resulting intervals would contain the true mean’
  • Which are wrong, and what exactly is wrong with each?

Try it: the answer

  • A. Wrong, despite sounding careful: ‘confident’ is standing in for ‘probability’, the same error as B
  • B. Wrong: the true mean is not a random variable with a 95% chance of falling anywhere. It is fixed
  • C. Right: the coverage statement, about the procedure across repeated samples, not about this one interval
  • Keep C’s wording. It is the one worth writing on an exam

Block 2: comparing groups, and how big a study needs to be

Putting the interval to work, before the study even starts

One mean is rarely the question

  • We can put an interval round one mean, the basis of a descriptive study
  • The analytic studies make comparisons: men against women, treated against untreated
  • And before any of that, how large does the study need to be?

An interval for a difference in means

  • The estimate is the difference between two groups’ means
    • The standard error combines the two groups’ own
  • This works because two things are true
    • Each group’s mean has an approximately normal sampling distribution, which the CLT delivered earlier this block
    • The difference between two normal quantities is itself normal. We take this as given
  • Once the difference is normal, it takes the same estimate ± 1.96 SE treatment as anything else. Nothing new has to be learnt here

Does cholesterol differ between women and men?

Quantity Estimate 95% CI p
Cholesterol, women minus men, mmol/L 0.169 0.147 to 0.19 < 0.001
  • Check the direction before reading the interval: the software’s own output names which group was subtracted from which

An interval for a difference in proportions

  • Same argument as the difference in means, with one qualification
    • For a proportion the normal shape is an approximation, not an exact consequence, since the underlying variable takes only two values
    • The approximation is good when both groups have a reasonable number of events and non-events, and poor when either is small
  • Exact methods exist for when it is small. The lab names them

Streptomycin: 69% against 33% improved

Quantity Estimate 95% CI p
Improved, streptomycin minus control 0.364 0.169 to 0.559 < 0.001
  • The same trial we met in Week 7

Whether the interval includes zero

  • This interval excludes zero
  • Zero is not among the values these data sit well with, the same compatibility reading from Block 1
  • Reading that as a real difference is right, and Week 9 makes it formal, with a proper hypothesis test
  • The interval is more informative than a test that only presents a p-value

What makes an interval wide or narrow

  • Three things decide an interval’s width
    • How variable the data are
    • How many observations there are
    • How much confidence is being claimed
  • Only the second is usually in the researcher’s gift

Width and the square root of n

  • The width shrinks with one over the square root of n: steeply at first, then flattening
  • The two Try it points from earlier sit on this same curve: a hundredfold increase in n bought a tenfold gain in precision

What happens to the interval when the sample shrinks?

Sample n 95% CI Width
Full 39176 4.963 to 4.984 0.022
50% subset 19588 4.956 to 4.986 0.031
10% subset 3917 4.934 to 5.001 0.067
  • Cut the sample in half, the interval gets about 40% wider. Cut it to a tenth, roughly three times wider

Try it: how much bigger, to halve the interval?

  • To halve the width of a confidence interval, what has to happen to n?
  • The half-width is 1.96 × SE, and SE = SD / sqrt(n)
  • One line, two minutes

Try it: the answer

  • Quadruple it
  • Width = 2 × 1.96 × SD / sqrt(n): n only enters through its square root
  • Halving the width means doubling sqrt(n), and doubling a square root means multiplying n by 4
  • At n = 100 the real width is 4.27 hours/day. At n = 400 it’s 2.14, exactly half
  • Precision is bought with n, and bought slowly

From precision to study size

  • Four linked quantities
    • The effect size worth detecting
    • The variability
    • The sample size
    • The power
  • Fix any three, and the fourth follows

Power, defined

  • The Week 3 seminar named power, alongside allocation concealment and blinding
  • Power: the probability of detecting an effect of a given size, if it is really there

What power depends on

  • Power rises with a larger effect to detect, a bigger sample, or less variable data
  • Power falls when a stricter significance level is demanded (Week 9 formalises this 5% threshold)

\[n \approx \frac{7.8 \times \text{variability}}{\text{gap}^2}\]

  • 7.8 is what 80% power at the 5% significance level costs
  • The gap is squared, so it moves n harder than anything else: halve it, and n roughly quadruples

How many per arm?

Proportion, control arm Proportion, treatment arm n per arm
0.33 0.69 29
  • For a gap the size of streptomycin’s, about 29 patients per arm
  • The formula above gives about 26 per arm. R’s own exact calculation gives the 29 used throughout, the number the lab reproduces

Try it: a smaller effect, how many more?

Control arm Treatment arm Gap
0.33 0.45 0.12
  • The streptomycin gap was 0.36, and 29 per arm detected it
  • Suppose the gap were a third the size, 0.12. Does the required n roughly triple, or something else?
  • Guess a number first. Three minutes

Try it: the answer

  • About 258 per arm: nearly nine times as many, not three times
  • Required n scales with roughly the inverse square of the effect size, the same shape as the width curve, seen from the other end

Why post-hoc power tells you nothing

  • Power asks how likely a study is to detect an effect, before it runs
  • Once the data are in, the estimate and its interval already say what the study could resolve
  • Power computed from the observed effect is just a re-expression of the p-value
  • A wide interval is the honest way to report a study that was too small
  • A common request from supervisors and reviewers, and one worth declining, for these reasons

What a protocol has to state

  • Four things
    • The effect size assumed, and where it came from
    • The variability assumed
    • The power and significance level
    • The resulting n per arm
  • A power calculation is only valid for the analysis it was actually done for
    • A calculation for comparing two proportions does not licence a study analysed by regression with three covariates
  • Sample size and power are the same calculation, solved for different unknowns, and both sit downstream of the analysis plan

When the approximation breaks down

  • The approximations above hold when the counts are not tiny, the same qualification from the difference-in-proportions slide
  • Exact methods exist for when they are. R has both, and the lab shows which function to reach for

Application

Judging the interpretation in somebody else’s writing

Three claims, three intervals

  • Each result below is real: the paper’s own reported statistic, and its own conclusion, quoted exactly, with a link to the paper
  • In pairs: does each sentence say what the interval supports? Where it does not, what would you write instead? Ten minutes
Study Result Paper's conclusion Link
Cao et al. (2020), NEJM 28-day mortality 19.2% vs 25.0%, difference -5.8pp (95% CI -17.3 to 5.7) '...no benefit was observed with lopinavir-ritonavir treatment beyond standard care.' PubMed
Radonovich et al. (2019), JAMA Influenza 8.2% vs 7.2% of person-seasons, difference 1.0pp (95% CI -0.5 to 2.5), p = 0.18 '...resulted in no significant difference in the incidence of laboratory-confirmed influenza.' PubMed
Williamson et al. (2020), Nature HR 1.48 (1.29 to 1.69) Black; 1.45 (1.32 to 1.58) South Asian, vs white 'Black and South Asian people were at higher risk, even after adjustment for other factors.' PubMed
  • A hazard ratio (Williamson) is a ratio measure like the RR and OR from Week 2, with a null value of 1. Week 9 formalises it

Three claims, judged

  • Cao et al.: too imprecise to support ‘no benefit’
    • The interval runs from a 17-point reduction in mortality to a 6-point increase, compatible with both a large benefit and real harm
    • The trial’s own next sentence half-admits this
  • Radonovich et al.: precise, but the sentence is potentially misleading
    • The narrow interval already shows any difference either way is small. ‘No significant difference’ throws that away
    • Cao’s imprecision and this one point in opposite directions: one interval is too wide to conclude ‘no difference’, the other narrow enough to conclude it
    • Source: Hemming and Taljaard, Medical Journal of Australia 2021
  • Williamson et al.: sound
    • The interval is well away from 1, and the claim matches it, correctly hedged
    • The honest question is what the estimate was adjusted for, not whether the arithmetic is right

Key points, and what the lab does

  • An estimate from a sample is not the population value. The interval says how far off it might be
  • The SD describes the data. The SE describes the estimate
  • An interval is the range of values the data are compatible with, and it covers the population value 95% of the time in the long run: not the same as being 95% sure about this one
  • Precision is bought with n, and bought slowly
  • The lab builds every one of these intervals in R, on nhanes_mph and strep_tb, and reconciles the hand calculations from today against the software’s answers
